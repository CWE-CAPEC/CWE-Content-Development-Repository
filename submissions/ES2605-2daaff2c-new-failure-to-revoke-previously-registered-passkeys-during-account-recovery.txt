NAME: Failure to Revoke Previously Registered Passkeys During Account Recovery

DESCRIPTION:

Modern applications increasingly support passwordless authentication using
passkeys based on WebAuthn/FIDO2. Passkeys are intentionally designed to
operate independently from passwords using asymmetric cryptography, where:

- the private key remains on the user's device,
- the public key is stored server-side,
- and authentication occurs through cryptographic challenge signing rather
than password verification.

Because of this architecture, password resets do not automatically
invalidate previously enrolled passkeys. This behavior is commonly
considered “by design” within the WebAuthn ecosystem.

However, the weakness described here is not related to broken
cryptography, authentication bypass, or flaws in the WebAuthn standard
itself. The issue instead lies in how many applications currently
implement account recovery and compromise remediation flows around
persistent passkey authenticators.

A common user expectation during account recovery is:

- resetting the password,
- reviewing suspicious activity,
- revoking active sessions,
- or selecting “logout all devices”

should remove unauthorized access from previously compromised
accounts.

In practice, many applications continue trusting previously enrolled
passkeys after these recovery actions without:

- prominently informing the user,
- forcing review of registered passkeys,
- distinguishing passkeys from normal sessions,
- or clearly communicating that attacker-controlled authenticators may still remain active.

This creates a security gap between:

1. the technical behavior of passkeys, and
2. the security expectations created by recovery workflows.

The weakness becomes particularly important in real-world compromise
scenarios involving temporary account access.

Example attack scenario:

1. An attacker temporarily compromises a victim’s account through
phishing, session theft, malware, social engineering, or physical
device access.
2. The attacker registers their own device as a passkey authenticator.
3. The victim later detects suspicious activity and performs standard recovery actions such as:
     - changing the password,
     - logging out sessions/devices,
     - reviewing suspicious activity,
     - or initiating account recovery workflows.
4. The victim reasonably believes the account has been secured.
5. However, the attacker may still authenticate using the previously
enrolled passkey because the passkey remains trusted independently of
the password.

From a technical perspective, this behavior may be
intentional. However, from a security design and recovery integrity
perspective, the problem is that:

- the attacker persistence mechanism survives recovery,
- users are not clearly informed,
- and the recovery workflow may communicate a misleading sense of remediation completeness.

This weakness effectively creates a form of long-term persistence
through trusted authenticators.

The issue is especially relevant because passkeys are intentionally designed to be:
- phishing resistant,
- difficult to revoke accidentally,
- persistent across devices/ecosystems,
- and treated as high-trust authenticators.

As adoption of passwordless authentication increases, this persistence
model introduces new account recovery risks that differ significantly
from traditional password-based systems.

Potential consequences include:

- incomplete account recovery,
- persistent unauthorized access after remediation,
- reduced effectiveness of password-reset-based incident response,
- misleading security assumptions by users,
- difficulty identifying attacker-controlled authenticators and long-term attacker persistence through trusted passkeys.

This weakness does not require broken authentication
controls. Instead, it emerges from insufficient authenticator
lifecycle transparency during security-sensitive recovery operations.

The severity of this issue depends heavily on user expectations and
implementation details. In environments where recovery flows strongly
imply that “all unauthorized access has been removed,” persistent
passkey authenticators may undermine the integrity of the recovery
process itself.

This issue may commonly affect:

- WebAuthn/FIDO2 passkeys,
- passwordless authentication systems,
- hardware security keys,
- and persistent authenticators independent from passwords.

Possible mitigations include:

- clearly warning users that passkeys remain active after password reset,
- requiring passkey review during suspicious activity remediation,
- optionally revoking passkeys during high-risk recovery events,
- or introducing stronger visibility into previously registered authenticators.

Therefore, this weakness arises when applications fail to properly
communicate or manage persistent passkey authenticators during account
recovery, potentially allowing attacker persistence after users
believe compromise remediation has completed.
