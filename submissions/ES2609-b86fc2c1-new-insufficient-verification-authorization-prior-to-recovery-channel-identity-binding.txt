SUBMISSION TYPE: Software

NAME: Insufficient Verification of Authorization Prior to Recovery-Channel Identity Binding (PEW - Phantom Entity Weaponization)

DESCRIPTION:

This weakness occurs when a system permits a recovery or contact channel
(such as an email address) to be reassigned to an account without
requiring authorization from the real owner of that channel, and the
subsequent claim or verification step treats successful interaction with
the channel as sufficient proof that the reassignment was legitimate.

The weakness is a failure to distinguish two separate facts that a
correct system must verify independently: (1) whether the party
completing a claim/verification action actually controls the channel in
question, and (2) whether the assignment of that channel to the account
being verified was itself authorized by the channel's real owner. A
system exhibiting this weakness only ever checks the first fact. It
never checks the second, and silently treats confirmation of the first
as if it also confirmed the second.

This is a design-level authorization gap, not an implementation defect
in the verification mechanism itself: the mechanism that confirms
channel possession (e.g., a verification link or code sent to the
channel) may function exactly as intended. The flaw is that the system
was never designed to ask whether the party who initiated the
channel-to-account assignment had the standing to do so on behalf of the
channel's real owner. As a result, an assignment made without the real
owner's knowledge or consent is treated as validated the moment the real
owner performs an ordinary, good-faith interaction with the channel -
such as completing what they believe is their own registration or
resolving a routine account notification.

This should concern developers and designers because the resulting
binding is not transient or easily detected: once the claim/verification
step completes, the system has no further signal indicating anything
irregular occurred, since every individual action involved (assigning a
channel value, dispatching a verification communication, and the
recipient's interaction with that communication) appears legitimate in
isolation. The weakness is only observable in the composition of these
steps, not in any single step examined alone, which makes it difficult
to catch through standard code review or unit-level security testing
focused on individual functions.

The technical impact is the unauthorized and typically irreversible
binding of a real identity's verified channel to an account or resource
the channel's owner did not choose and has no relationship to. Depending
on system design, this can result in denial of the channel owner's
ability to independently use that channel elsewhere on the same
platform, unauthorized attribution of a verified real-world identity to
an account controlled by another party, or, where the party completing
the initial reassignment also controls the destination channel, full
unauthorized control of the affected account.

A significant variation of this weakness is that it does not require the
channel's real owner to hold any account, privilege, or prior
relationship with the system, the account being modified, or the actor
who performed the reassignment. The verification step's failure to check
authorization of the assignment applies equally whether the real owner
is a first-time visitor to the system or an established user of an
entirely separate, unrelated account or organization within the same
platform, since the missing check is agnostic to the claimant's prior
context.

A further variation involves the party who performs the initial
reassignment already possessing legitimate authorization to modify the
account being reassigned to (for example, modifying an account they
already own or administer). In such cases, no separate authorization
failure occurs at the reassignment step itself; the weakness is confined
entirely to the claim-acceptance step's failure to verify that the new
channel's real owner consented to the binding, independent of whether
the party initiating the change had standing over the account.

This weakness is distinct from failures where the same party is both the
account holder and the party harmed by an unverified change to their own
credentials, since here the party harmed by the unauthorized binding is
never the party who held authorization over the account being modified.
It is likewise distinct from workflow-sequencing failures confined to a
single actor's own transaction, since the defining characteristic here
is that an uninvolved party's ordinary, good-faith interaction with a
legitimate system communication is what completes the unauthorized
binding, without that party ever being aware a reassignment occurred.
