SUBMISSION TYPE: Software

NAME: Insufficient Verification of Recorded Authorization Decision

DESCRIPTION:

The product accepts a previously recorded authorization decision as
permission to perform an action. Examples of such decisions are a human
approval, a code-review vote, a deployment approval, a completed approval
task, or a delegated grant. The product does not verify two properties
before acting on the decision. First, that the decision was issued by an
entity with authority to make it. Second, that the decision applies to the
exact action, arguments, target, execution, and time window in which it is
being used.

The weakness occurs when the moment a decision is made is separated from
the moment it is enforced. The decision is stored as data in a checkpoint,
session, database row, task record, review record, or event history, and is
read back later. If the product treats the stored value as the decision
itself, the stored value becomes the only evidence of authorization. It
describes a decision but does not prove one was made, by whom, or for what.

Two distinct failures follow.

Issuer not verified. Any principal with write access to the stored decision
can create one. Write access to operational state is routinely held by
components that have no authority to approve the action, such as migration
jobs, recovery services, backup agents, sidecars, integration accounts, and
scoped database roles. The product cannot distinguish a decision written by
the authorized approval path from one written by any of these components.
Binding not verified. A genuine decision made by an authorized party is
applied to something it was never issued for. This happens when the
arguments of the approved action change after approval, when the approved
object changes after review (new commits, a changed target branch, a
modified plan), when the decision is reused in another execution or after
its intended lifetime, or when it is consumed by a downstream system for a
different action.

In both cases the product performs the action under its own identity and
privileges. The principal that caused the action never holds the
credentials the action uses and never authenticates as the approver. This
is a confused-deputy condition. The product's records then attribute the
action to an approver who either never acted or approved something else.
The weakness therefore defeats the authorization control and also corrupts
the evidence that the control operated, which breaks accountability and
non-repudiation.

A related form affects the requirement rather than the decision. When the
rule that decides whether a decision is required (approval policy, required
approvers, protected-resource settings, trusted-action lists) is itself
stored in state that a lower-privileged principal can modify, that
principal can remove the requirement. The action then executes with no
decision and no record that one was required.

The weakness appears across technologies that separate decision from
enforcement: AI agent frameworks with human-in-the-loop tool approval,
durable execution and workflow engines with pause and resume, BPMN user
tasks, CI/CD manual approvals and protected deployment environments,
code-review merge rules, identity governance approval workflows, and
maker-checker or four-eyes controls in financial systems.

This entry differs from related entries as follows. CWE-345 covers
unverified authenticity of data in general but does not identify the
authorization decision as the critical artifact or require binding to
action, target, and time. CWE-807 covers security decisions that rely on
attacker-modifiable input, but does not cover an authentic decision applied
to the wrong action, target, or time. CWE-863 describes an incorrect
authorization outcome without naming the cause. Vulnerabilities with this
root cause are currently mapped inconsistently across CWE-284, CWE-472,
CWE-841, CWE-862, CWE-863, and CWE-1280.

Products avoid this weakness when the evidence accepted at enforcement is
produced by an authority the state writer cannot write, and is bound to the
action it authorizes. Examples are a signed decision artifact covering
issuer, action, argument digest, target, execution identifier, expiry, and
single-use nonce, verified immediately before the action runs, a
per-execution task token issued and validated by the service, an
identity-bound vote recorded and enforced by the server, and approvals
automatically invalidated when the approved object changes.
